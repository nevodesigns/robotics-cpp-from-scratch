## Symptom

An arm that reaches every target correctly and occasionally swings through
something on the way, or folds into a posture that hits its own base, or puts
the elbow exactly where the operator is standing.

The tool always arrives. The route it takes is sometimes indefensible, and
nothing in the code chose it.

## Cause

A planar two link arm reaching a point almost always has **two** answers, and
the solver returned one.

Both are correct. Elbow up and elbow down put the tool in exactly the same
place, and the arithmetic prefers neither: `acos` answers between zero and pi,
so it hands back one of them, and the other is its negation.

```cpp
const double q2 = std::acos(cos_q2);   // one of two, and which one is arbitrary
```

Taking that as the answer means the choice between the two postures is being
made by the sign convention of a library function, which knows nothing about the
table, the base or the operator.

The same shape appears everywhere in robotics, and usually with more than two
answers. A six axis arm has up to eight. A redundant arm has infinitely many.
The number changes and the lesson does not: **an inverse problem returns a set,
and picking from it is a separate decision that belongs to the caller.**

## Fix

Return both, and say when they have merged:

```cpp
struct Solutions {
  ArmSolution elbow_up;
  ArmSolution elbow_down;
  bool coincide = false;
};
```

Then choose deliberately, on grounds the maths cannot know:

- Which is nearer the arm's current configuration, so it moves least.
- Which keeps the elbow away from the obstacle, or the person.
- Which stays inside the joint limits, since a mathematically valid answer can
  ask for an angle the joint does not have.

Two properties worth testing, because a solver can fail either way.

The two answers must be **genuinely different**, or the solver is returning one
under two names and the caller's careful choice does nothing. Checking that the
elbow angles have opposite signs is enough.

And they must **coincide** where they should. At full extension and at the inner
limit the two really are one answer, and reporting that is useful: it is exactly
where a small move of the target demands a large move of the joints. See
`E-KIN-0002`.
