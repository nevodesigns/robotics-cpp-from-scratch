## Symptom

A calibration is done carefully and the square closes to within a few centimetres, and it never gets better than that,
however many times it is repeated. Or two people calibrate the same robot and get numbers that differ in the third place,
and each makes the robot better.

## Cause

The calibration is as good as the measurements of the moves, and they are made from outside the robot, with a tape and a
protractor or a camera. The robot cannot measure how far it went, since that is the thing it is wrong about.

Measured, twenty calibrations of a robot whose left wheel is 1.2 % big, right 0.6 % small and wheelbase 2 % wide, with a
tape good to 3 mm on five metres and a protractor good to 0.2 degrees:

| scale | rms error | worst |
|---|---|---|
| left wheel | 0.0006 | 0.0015 |
| right wheel | 0.0006 | 0.0013 |
| wheel base | 0.0007 | 0.0016 |

Each is right to about a tenth of a percent. And the 16 metre square:

| odometry believing | position error | heading error |
|---|---|---|
| the drawing | 249.9 cm | 46.4 deg |
| the calibration | 2.3 cm | 0.4 deg |

A hundred times better in position, and what is left is the noise in the three numbers.

## Fix

Make the test moves long, so a fixed error in the measurement is a smaller fraction: a straight drive of five metres turns a
3 mm error into 0.06 percent, and a spin of four turns spreads a fifth of a degree over 1440. Repeat it and average. And
remember what it cannot do: this finds the systematic errors, the ones that are the same every time. A wheel that slips on a
wet floor is a different robot each time, and the calibration of a dry one is no help.
