## Symptom

A sensor value is, once in a while, nonsense: a pose that jumps across the room
for one frame, a velocity of a million. The link reports no checksum failure at
the time, and the counters look healthy. It happens a few times an hour and never
when anyone is watching.

## Cause

A checksum is a small number, and a wrong frame can match it by luck. For an
eight bit checksum the odds are one in 256 for a frame that is wrong in a random
way.

Dropped bytes produce exactly that kind of frame. The parser takes the bytes it
was promised from the wrong place, so the bytes it checks are a **misaligned
slice** of the stream, which to the checksum is arbitrary data. Measured, at one
dropped byte in a hundred:

| | misaligned frames | failed the checksum | passed it, wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| ten thousand frames | 876 | 873 | 3 |

876 divided by 256 is 3.4, and three got through.

A flipped bit does not do this. CRC-8 detects **every** single bit error by
construction, so 1113 flips produced no wrong frame at all. The two kinds of
damage need different defences, and a test that only flips bits finds nothing.

## Fix

Make the check wider, so luck is rarer: CRC-16 turns one in 256 into one in
65536, which is 256 times fewer wrong frames at the cost of one byte. Lesson
08-07 measures the difference.

Then make a wrong frame survivable rather than only improbable. Check a value
against what the robot could have done: a velocity above the motor's limit is
wrong however good its checksum was. Phase 19 returns to this as a rule about
trusting nothing from a wire.
