## Symptom

A gravity compensation that works over part of the range and fights the arm over
the rest. Or an arm that holds its pose with a gain of 50 and falls over the top
with a gain of 10, with nothing else changed, and a gain that is fine for an arm
hanging down is not enough for the same arm standing up.

## Cause

Three separate errors with the same root: gravity's pull is not constant. It is a
cosine of the pose.

**The sign.** The torque is the weight times the cosine of the link's angle to the
horizontal, so it falls to zero at vertical and reverses beyond it. A
compensation fitted to the forward half of the range, or written as a positive
constant, pushes the wrong way once the arm leans back. Mirrored across the
vertical, the torque at 0.3 rad and at pi minus 0.3 rad are equal and opposite.

**The wrong angle.** The elbow torque depends on the angle the second link makes
with the horizontal, which is the shoulder angle plus the elbow angle, and on
neither alone. The same elbow torque appears at (0.3, 0.5) and (0.8, 0.0).

**A hilltop.** At the top the torque falls as the arm leans further, so gravity
adds to any lean instead of resisting it. Measured, it pushes the shoulder over by
17.6 N m for every radian. A proportional gain below that cannot bring the arm
back, and one above it can:

| gain | where an upright arm nudged 0.02 rad ends |
|---|---|
| 10 | 3.28 rad, fallen over |
| 20 | 1.79 rad |
| 50 | 1.60 rad, held |

## Fix

Compute the torque from the pose each time, from the angle each link makes with the
horizontal, and check it against something that shares no algebra with it: the
slope of the potential energy, which the lesson's test differentiates numerically
across 507 poses and payloads and agrees to 5e-9 N m. Choose the gain against the
largest slope gravity reaches at the poses the arm will hold, which is where it
is upright with its payload, and not against the pose it hangs in.
