title: The size of a struct taken as the sum of the sizes of its members
match: the end of a struct is padded too, because an array of them has to stay aligned
match: rounding up to a multiple of the alignment
platforms: linux, windows
teaches: 02-13-what-a-struct-looks-like-in-memory

## Symptom

A buffer is allocated as `count * (sizeof(a) + sizeof(b))`, because that is what a record holds, and a loop that fills it runs off the end. Or a binary file
written by one program is read by another that computed the record length the same way, and every record after the first is shifted.

## Cause

`sizeof` of a struct is not the sum of its members. The members are padded between, and the struct is padded at the **end** as well, to a multiple of its own
alignment, which is the largest of its members'. It has to be, because in an array the second element starts where the first ends, and its members have to be
aligned too.

Measured, a `uint64_t` and a `uint8_t`: nine bytes of members and a size of **16**. The test puts three in an array and checks that the second starts exactly
`sizeof` after the first and that its `uint64_t` sits on a multiple of eight.

The placement itself is arithmetic. Each member starts at the next offset that is a multiple of its alignment, which is `(offset + alignment - 1) / alignment
* alignment`, and the integer division throwing away the remainder is what makes it a rounding up. The lesson writes it and checks every offset against the
compiler's own `offsetof`, for structs of mixed members and one with an `alignas(16)` member.

## Fix

Use `sizeof` of the struct, always, and never a sum. And if the bytes are going outside the process, to a file or a wire, do not send the struct at all:
lesson 08-01's encoder writes each field at a stated offset, which is the same on every machine.
