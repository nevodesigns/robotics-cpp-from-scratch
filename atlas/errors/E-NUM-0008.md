## Symptom

A numerical derivative is not accurate enough, so the step is made smaller and
it gets worse. Smaller again and it is worse again, and eventually it is zero,
or noise, or out by the whole of the answer.

Everything about the calculus is right.

## Cause

Two errors pull in opposite directions and only one of them is in the calculus.

**Truncation.** A difference quotient is the slope of a chord, not a tangent,
and the gap between them shrinks with the step. That is the error people expect.

**Cancellation.** `f(x + h)` and `f(x - h)` are nearly equal, and subtracting
two nearly equal doubles throws away the leading digits they had in common.
That error *grows* as the step shrinks, because there is less and less
difference to keep.

Measured on `sin(x)e^(0.3x)` at `x = 1.2`, the error in the derivative:

| step | forward | central | complex step |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1e-2 | 4.546e-03 | 2.576e-05 | 2.576e-05 |
| 1e-6 | 4.518e-07 | **2.055e-12** | 2.578e-13 |
| 1e-8 | **1.388e-08** | 8.329e-09 | 2.220e-16 |
| 1e-12 | 4.432e-04 | 1.101e-04 | 1.110e-16 |
| 1e-16 | **9.202e-01** | 9.202e-01 | 2.220e-16 |

The last row is not an approximation of anything. The true derivative is 0.920,
so an error of 0.920 means the answer has no correct digits at all.

Each method has a best step and it is where the theory says:

| method | best step measured | theory | best error |
|---|---|---|---|
| forward | 1e-8 | sqrt(eps) = 1.5e-8 | 1.388e-08 |
| central | 1e-6 | cbrt(eps) = 6.1e-6 | 2.055e-12 |

## Fix

**Use a central difference.** One extra call, and about six thousand times less
error at each method's own best step, because the errors on the two sides cancel
rather than add.

**Take the step from the epsilon rather than from a feeling.**

```cpp
const double h = std::cbrt(std::numeric_limits<double>::epsilon()) *
                 std::max(std::fabs(x), 1.0);
```

The scaling is `E-NUM-0009`.

**Divide by the span you got, not the one you meant.**

```cpp
const double above = x + h, below = x - h;
return (f(above) - f(below)) / (above - below);
```

`(x + h) - (x - h)` is not exactly `2h` in floating point, and using `2h` throws
away a digit for nothing.

**Where the function is yours to change, consider not subtracting at all.** For
a function that is analytic and written to work on complex arguments,

```cpp
return f(std::complex<double>(x, h)).imag() / h;
```

has no subtraction of nearly equal numbers anywhere in it, so there is nothing
to cancel and the step can be as small as you like. In the table above it
reaches one unit in the last place at 1e-8 and stays there at 1e-100.

That is a real technique with a real restriction: the function must be analytic
and must survive a complex argument, which rules out anything with `fabs`,
`min`, `max`, or a branch on the value, and it rules out a black box entirely.

And none of this is the right question for a derivative of **measured** data,
which is `E-MATH-0006`.
