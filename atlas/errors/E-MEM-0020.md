title: A struct a third bigger than its data, because of the order its members were written in

match: a byte, a double and a byte: the member order a person writes costs eight bytes in padding
platforms: linux, windows

## Symptom

A buffer of a million records is 48 MB and the data in it is 29 MB. Memory is tight on the board, or a log file is twice what it should be, or
a loop over an array of records is slower than the arithmetic in it says it should be, and nobody has looked at the struct.

## Cause

Each member sits at an address that is a multiple of its own alignment, usually its size, so a `double` sits at a multiple of eight. Whatever lies
between the end of one member and the start of the next is padding, and stays unused. Members are placed in the order they are written, so the order
decides how much padding there is.

Measured, a byte, a double and a byte:

| order | size | padding | offsets |
|---|---|---|---|
| as written | 24 | 14 | 0, 8, 16 |
| largest first | 16 | 6 | 0, 8, 9 |

And a telemetry record of eight members holding 29 bytes of data, as written and in the best order:

| order | size |
|---|---|
| in the order they occurred to somebody | 48 bytes, 19 of them padding |
| the best order | 32 bytes |

A million of them is 48 MB against 32, and a cache line holds 1.3 records against 2.0.

## Fix

Put the most strictly aligned members first. For alignments that are powers of two, which they always are, that leaves no gap before any of them, and the
rest fit wherever the last one ended. The lesson checks it against every possible order of 400 random structs of three to six members, and largest
alignment first gave the smallest size in all 400, saving up to 32 bytes.

It costs readability when the natural order groups related fields, so do it where it matters: a struct that is held in the millions, written to disk or
shared with another processor. Check the size with a `static_assert(sizeof(T) == 32)`, so a field added later cannot quietly cost 16 bytes.
