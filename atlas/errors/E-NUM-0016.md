## Symptom

An integrator is checked against "the same integrator with a much smaller step",
and past some point the measured error stops falling. Then it starts rising.
Taking a thousand times more steps in the reference makes the answer look worse.

The conclusion drawn is usually that the method has a floor. It does not; the
reference does.

## Cause

Every step rounds, and a reference that takes ten million steps has accumulated
ten million roundings.

Runge Kutta run for one second with increasing numbers of steps, against the
closed form answer:

| steps | step size | error |
|---|---|---|
| 100 | 1.00e-02 | 2.443e-09 |
| 1 000 | 1.00e-03 | 2.425e-13 |
| **10 000** | 1.00e-04 | **3.886e-16** |
| 100 000 | 1.00e-05 | 1.116e-14 |
| 1 000 000 | 1.00e-06 | 7.161e-15 |
| 10 000 000 | 1.00e-07 | 3.392e-14 |

The best reference took ten thousand steps. A thousand times more of them was
about ninety times worse.

It is the same V as the numerical derivative in `E-NUM-0008`, from the same two
errors pulling opposite ways: truncation falls with the step and rounding rises
with the number of them. Anywhere a smaller step is supposed to buy accuracy,
there is a floor and then a wall.

## Fix

**Test against a closed form wherever one exists.** A linear oscillator, a
constant acceleration, a body in free fall, a rotation about a fixed axis. These
are not toy problems, they are instruments: the answer is exact, so what you
measure is your method and nothing else.

**Where no closed form exists**, know the reference's own floor before drawing
conclusions from anything near it. Sweep the reference's step, find where its
error stops falling, and treat that as the noise floor of the whole experiment.
Reporting an error below your instrument's resolution is reporting the
instrument.

**And use a different method for the reference**, not the same one at a smaller
step. Two methods that agree are evidence; one method agreeing with itself is
mostly evidence that it is consistent.

The general form is worth keeping. **You cannot measure an error smaller than
your reference's own**, and a reference is a measuring instrument with a
resolution like any other.
