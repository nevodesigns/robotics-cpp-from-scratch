## Symptom

Odometry that is excellent on the test track and poor in the building. Or the
reverse: fine in the aisles and hopeless the moment it is asked to drive a long
corridor.

The parameters were checked. The robot was driven up and down and the estimate
matched a tape measure.

## Cause

**Which error matters depends on what the robot drives**, and a calibration run
that only drives one kind of path cannot see the errors the other kind exposes.

Measured over a hundred metres, on the same robot, changing only the path:

| error | driving straight | with turns |
|---|---|---|
| wheel radius 1% out | 1.0000 m | 1.1235 m |
| **wheel base 1% out** | **0.0000 m** | **1.0580 m** |
| initial heading 1 degree out | 1.7453 m | 0.1115 m |
| wheel slip, 1% typical | 1.5107 m | 0.9051 m |

Read the second row. A wheel base that is one percent wrong costs **nothing at
all** on a straight line, because the base only ever divides the *difference*
between the wheels, and driving straight that difference is zero. Drive the same
distance with turns in it and the same error costs over a metre.

The third row is the mirror image. A heading that starts one degree out costs
1.75 metres over a straight hundred, which is just the distance times the
tangent, and almost nothing around a closed loop, because the offset largely
cancels on the way back.

So a calibration that drives in a straight line measures the wheel radius
perfectly and says nothing whatsoever about the wheel base.

## Fix

Calibrate each parameter on the path that exposes it, which is what the standard
procedures do and why they look odd until you know this:

- **Wheel radius**, from a long straight run. Compare the distance the estimate
  reports against a tape measure.
- **Wheel base**, from turning in place or driving a square. Compare the heading
  the estimate reports against a full turn, or check whether a square closes.

Then test on a path that is **not** the calibration path, or the test only
confirms the thing you already fitted.

Two related habits.

Drive the square both ways round. A systematic error and a random one look the
same after one lap and separate after two in opposite directions, which is the
whole idea behind the standard bidirectional square test.

And record the residual drift per metre driven, rather than a total. A total
depends on how far you happened to drive; a rate is a property of the robot and
can be compared between runs and between machines.
