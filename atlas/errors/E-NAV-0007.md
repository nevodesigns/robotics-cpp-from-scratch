## Symptom

An innovation monitor is running, it has been healthy for months, and the
estimate is wrong anyway. Its numbers look correct: the residuals are the size
the filter expects and their mean is near zero.

The fault appears only when somebody drives the robot differently, and then the
monitor lights up for a completely unrelated reason.

## Cause

An innovation is the measurement minus the prediction. Both of those can be
wrong in the same way at the same time, and then their difference is fine.

That is exactly what a late measurement does. Each correction drags the estimate
back into the measurement's own past. Once the filter settles there, the next
measurement, also from that past, agrees with it. The two are consistent with
each other and neither is consistent with the world.

Measured, the normalised innovation over a forty second run with the fix fused
as current:

| fix age | steady 1 m/s | stop and go |
|---|---|---|
| 0.000 s | 0.921 | 0.921 |
| 0.080 s | 0.923 | 1.182 |
| 0.300 s | 0.927 | 2.679 |
| 0.600 s | 0.935 | 4.386 |

At a constant speed the monitor reports the same figure whether the fix is
current or two thirds of a second old, while the actual error goes from 3 cm to
59 cm. Changing speed changes the lag, and **a changing error is the only kind a
consistency check can see**.

## Fix

Keep the monitor, and know what it is for.

**Compare it against its own healthy baseline**, not against the theoretical 1.0.
The runs above sit at 0.92 when everything is right, because of the particular
prior, the fusion rate and the noise. A monitor is a change detector, so the
number that matters is the one it reported on a run you trusted.

**Keep the mean and the spread separately.** They fail differently: a filter
alternating between two large opposite errors has a mean that looks perfect and
an rms that does not.

```cpp
monitor.add(rc::nav::innovation_of(filter.estimate(), measurement));
if (monitor.rms() > baseline * 2.0) raise();
```

**Exercise the thing you want to test.** A consistency check is blind to
anything constant, so the acceptance run has to include acceleration, stops and
turns. A robot driven at one speed in a straight line will pass tests it should
fail, and this applies well beyond estimation: a steady state hides every error
that is proportional to a rate of change.

**And do not rely on it alone.** It uses only what the filter already has. Some
outside reference, however occasional, is the only thing that can catch an error
the filter and its sensors agree on.
